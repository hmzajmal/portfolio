# Xiangqi.com

Chinese chess, played online against people or bots. Retention was 20% and the business wanted ten times the players. Two redesigns of the lobby took conversion from 1.79% to 11%.

**Role**

Product Designer, Research

**Team**

8, at Arbisoft

**Duration**

Jan 2021 to Dec 2022

**Live**

play.xiangqi.com

01/The problem

## People signed up, played once, and left.

Xiangqi.com let you register for free, chat, and play against other people or bots at different levels. Only one in five players came back. The business goal was ten times the user base in a year, and the product could not get there while losing four in five.

When I joined, the team was eight people and almost all the product decisions had been made by engineers, because shipping features had been the priority. My first job was to find out why players left, and to put it in a form the product manager could act on.

![The original lobby](/work/xiangqi/old-lobby-ui.png)

_Caption:_ Before. The lobby players saw when I joined.

02/The research

## Ask the players, then read the competition.

We added a one-question rating prompt after every finished game. The answers pointed at three things. The screen showed too much during play. Players could not find games with the timer and rules they wanted. And the most valuable feature, inviting a friend to play, was hidden so well most people did not know it existed.

I compared Chess.com, Lichess and TianTian on what they offer, who they serve, and where their own users complain, then read through the Play Store and App Store reviews for all of them. Chess players had already solved most of our lobby problems. There was no reason to solve them again from scratch.

From that I drew a new information architecture with the product manager, then two lobby layouts on paper. I priced both with the tech lead before choosing. The cheaper one to build was also the clearer one, so that was an easy call.

03/Version 2

## The redesign everyone liked, and the data did not.

Version 2 shipped with a clean hierarchy and a lobby built around live games. Players and stakeholders liked it. The company held a tournament to celebrate.

Then we watched it for four months with heatmaps, and three problems showed up. Outside peak hours there were not enough live games, so the lobby looked empty and dead. The New Game button did not stand out, so people did not know how to start. And matchmaking was slow, so players gave up waiting and played bots instead, which is a lonelier game.

The design was right for a busy lobby. Most of the day, the lobby was not busy.

04/Version 3

## Show the board first.

Version 3 leads with a game board, not a list of games. A new visitor sees what Xiangqi looks like before they see how many people are online. The lobby was redrawn so it holds up with three players or three hundred, and New Game became the one obvious action on the page. The sign-up page got the same treatment: fewer fields, one path.

The same idea carried to the mobile app, which I designed in parallel with the web lobby.

05/Outcome

## 1.79% to 11%.

After version 3 shipped, the conversion rate the team tracked went from 1.79% to 11%. The Android app passed 100,000 downloads on the Play Store. Retention moved up from the 20% baseline, though I do not have the final figure, so I will not put a number on it.

The lesson I still use: a design that looks right in a full room can fail in an empty one. Design for the quiet hours, and check the heatmap before you celebrate.
